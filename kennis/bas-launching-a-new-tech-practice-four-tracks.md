# Bas's method: launch a new technology practice on four parallel tracks

When Bas advises an IT service provider (MSP / reseller) to stand up a brand-new technology practice — for example, replacing an aging on-premises stack with a cloud desktop-virtualization offering built on a vendor management layer — he structures the whole engagement around four parallel tracks rather than treating it as a single technical project. Each track has its own owner, next steps and planning.

1. **Technical capability** — assemble a small team of engineers, get an own (lab/demo) environment live, define exactly which knowledge is needed and to what depth, draw up an overview of trainings to follow and plan the exams. The first hands-on workshops should be done early so the team learns by doing, not by certifying first.

2. **Proposition / ROI** — turn the new capability into a clear service offering. He frames this explicitly as a cost-and-ROI conversation, not a feature list: define the services, build a cost model, and prepare a head-to-head comparison against the incumbent technology being replaced. Cover both onboarding existing clients and winning new ones.

3. **Sales / go-to-market** — start with the existing book of business as the low-hanging fruit (existing, often dissatisfied, clients on the incumbent platform are the warmest leads), then layer on lead generation, a marketing campaign, news/announcements, social media and a round-table.

4. **Internal community & profile** — identify the two or three people internally who genuinely have interest, push them to the foreground and coach them toward community status: events, presenting, blogging. A new practice also needs a renewed market profile, revived events with a different approach that involve clients, and expert sessions.

The insight worth reusing: Bas does not let an IT-services firm treat a new offering as 'just' a tech ramp-up. Capability, proposition, sales and visibility are built at the same time, with a recurring (e.g. biweekly) steering cadence keeping the tracks aligned. He also separates the commercial entry point (one strategically attractive technology to lead with) from the longer-term breadth ('later there's room for more').
