# Newsletter: Human idea first, AI assist (the 3am edition)

Essay edition, mid June 2026. Opens with a "true story": awake at 3am because his wife farts in her sleep, he realizes no AI model knew that until now. From there: AI only knows what we gave it; the idea has to be human. Backed by the MIT study "Your Brain on ChatGPT" and its cognitive-debt finding. Announces an upcoming podcast nugget on cognitive offloading. The follow-up edition (sleep talking) refers back to this one.

The paste contained this edition twice, identical; stored once.

## Core points
- AI knows what we gave it and not much more: the undocumented fix, the 3am thought, the thing in your environment that contradicts the docs.
- This piece itself: idea from a broken night, words typed on his phone with typos, AI cleaned up spelling afterwards. "The thinking is still me."
- MIT, Your Brain on ChatGPT: 54 people, about four months, three groups (ChatGPT, search engine, no tools). No-tools group had the strongest brain connectivity; 83 percent of the AI group could not quote a sentence from their own essay. Researchers called it cognitive debt.
- The flip: people who thought first and got the tool later showed higher recall and engagement. Same tool, opposite outcome, only the order differed.
- AI cannot lie awake or be bored, and boredom is where ideas come from. Filling every gap with AI drains the pool.
- Juniors: let them wrestle with the incident or design doc first, then use AI to pressure-test. "The first route produces output, the second one produces a senior, eventually."

## Full text (as published)

> True story. Stay with me, this ends up somewhere useful, I promise. I am a bad sleeper. Always have been. And my wife, lovely as she is, farts in her sleep. I mean, it happens. Not loud, the sound is not the problem, but, you know... it gets me every time. Nothing I can do about it, and nothing she can do about it either, she is asleep after all.
>
> So, there I am, awake at three in the morning. I start thinking. Not about my day, or whatever is waiting for me the next morning, I don't have that much to worry about, life is good and so is work. No, I think about ideas, topics, things I want to research or build, and sometimes, depending on the time, I get up and start.
>
> This time, somewhere between the fart and falling back asleep, the thought was this: AI did not know any of this. Until last night, no model on the planet knew this about my wife. And if one did, I sure didn't tell it.
>
> **What AI doesn't know**
> We talk about AI as if there is little left it doesn't know. It reads the internet, all the books it can get its "hands" on, documentation, code, so what else could there be?
>
> Well, a ton. Everything that never got written down. The thought you had at 3am and kept to yourself. The fix a colleague applied years ago and never documented (we all know that colleague). The thing you noticed in your environment last week that contradicts what the docs say.
>
> AI knows what we gave it, and not much more. The ideas, the experiences, the 3am thoughts, those still come from us. And that is exactly how I like to use it: the idea has to be human, AI can assist.
>
> This piece is a small example of that. The idea came from a fart in the dark, the words came from me, on my phone in my "notes" app, typos and all, and trust me, there were typos. English is not my first language, and at three in the morning it is barely my second. However, what AI did do is clean things up afterwards, the spelling, the typos, and more. The thinking is still me, and that's the important part.
>
> Which also means a model somewhere now knows about my wife. Sorry honey. Also a good test to see if she actually reads my work, my guess would be no. Well, sometimes, maybe.
>
> **Why the order matters (study)**
> That order, human idea first, tool second, is not just a personal preference, there is science behind it as well. I will cover it in an upcoming podcast nugget on cognitive offloading.
>
> BTW, go here for some of the other EUC News Nuggets Podcast episodes already on there, it's not much (yet) but a nice start. Spotify: https://shorturl.at/tOemw Apple podcast: https://shorturl.at/sR8ac
>
> A team at MIT ran a study called Your Brain on ChatGPT, 54 people writing essays over about four months, with brain scanners on and everything, split into three groups: one wrote with ChatGPT, one with a search engine, and one without any help but their own head and thoughts.
>
> The no-tools group showed the strongest and widest brain connectivity, the ChatGPT group the weakest, and 83 percent of the AI group could not quote a single sentence from the essay they had finished minutes earlier.
>
> They had produced the work, but they had not done the thinking, so almost nothing stuck. The researchers called it cognitive debt.
>
> **The clever part came at the end**
> They flipped the groups around, took the AI away from the people who had leaned on it the whole time, and handed it to the people who had done everything by hand.
>
> The former AI group struggled, there was not much to fall back on.
>
> The group that had done the hard thinking first and only then got the tool lit up, all showed higher recall, wider engagement. Same tool, opposite outcome, and the only difference was the order. I'd say it is also common sense.
>
> **One more thought.**
>
> AI can do a lot, but it cannot lie awake. It is never bored, never restless, never staring at the ceiling at 2am with nothing to do. And that is exactly where ideas come from. Boredom, friction, a broken night, the shower, a walk outside, the long drive home, those moments are the production engine for every original thought you have ever had.
>
> So if you let AI fill those moment, summarize every article, draft every first version, you are not just offloading work, you are also draining the pool your ideas come from. You see, boredom is a good thing. It might be the most underrated tool you own.
>
> **The lesson, and what it looks like in practice**
> The effort you remove with AI is very often the exact effort that was making you better. Skip it once, fine. Skip it every time, for years, and the skill never forms.
>
> We are still in the early stages so this doesn't have to become an issue, per se, but I doubt many will use Ai backwards as we have been discussing.
>
> Think of a junior on your team facing their first real incident, or their first design document. The tempting route is to let AI hand them the answer, fast and clean, and move on. The better route, let them wrestle with it first, form their own picture, get it half wrong, and only then bring in AI to pressure-test what they came up with. The first route produces output, the second one produces a senior, eventually.
>
> So no, I am probably never going to fix my sleep. But as long as the broken nights keep producing ideas, and the machine keeps cleaning up my English afterwards, I'll take the trade. Human idea, AI assist, in that order.
>
> My wife, by the way, denies everything.
>
> BvK.
