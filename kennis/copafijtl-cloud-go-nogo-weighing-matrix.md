# COPAFIJTL weighing matrix for a per-application cloud go/no-go

When Bas advises a (semi-)public-sector client on whether a given application or service should move to the cloud, he does not rely on the financial business case alone. He pairs his own go/no-go business case with a structured weighing matrix that scores each application against a fixed set of qualitative and quantitative axes, so the decision is navigable and defensible rather than a gut call. In a Dutch public-sector engagement (a shared-service collaboration of municipalities) the matrix used is COPAFIJTL, a known Dutch governmental acronym; Bas adopts the client's existing framework rather than imposing his own when a good one is already in play.

The nine axes:
- **Communicatie (Communication)** - is using the service in the cloud self-evident and frictionless for the end user?
- **Organisatie (Organisation)** - governance, and who has say over data ownership, support and functionality.
- **Personeel (Personnel)** - ease of use versus required training; is staff qualified, technically and functionally, to carry the responsibility and the management effort?
- **Administratieve organisatie (Administrative organisation)** - impact on work processes and procedures.
- **Financien (Finance)** - cost/benefit, efficiency and effectiveness, integrations, migration effort, synergy, cost structure.
- **Informatievoorziening (Information provision)** - quality, availability, fit into the architecture, information security.
- **Juridisch (Legal)** - laws, regulations and policy, privacy.
- **Technologie (Technology)** - market maturity, standards for integration and authorisation, hosting form (private/shared), quality of the service (performance, reliability, scalability).
- **Leverancier (Vendor qualifications)** - experience, reliability, references, security, contract terms.

Why Bas works this way:
- The matrix produces an **advisory input to the decision process**, not the decision itself. The owners/stakeholders of the application bring the weighed result forward; the matrix makes their reasoning explicit and repeatable.
- It operationalises his **"cloud, tenzij"** default: cloud is the preferred option, but every option is always tested against the criteria before it is chosen.
- It is deliberately **lightweight**: a fixed, finite set of axes you can fill in per application, not a heavy ISO-style apparatus. This mirrors his general stance that an assessment should be "good enough", not perfect.

Reusable takeaway: when the client has a credible existing decision framework, layer your business case onto it rather than replacing it. A short fixed-axis matrix turns a yes/no cloud question into a documented, auditable advice.
