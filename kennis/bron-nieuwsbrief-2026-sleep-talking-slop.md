# Newsletter: Sleep talking and AI slop

Essay edition, the week after the 3am edition (second half of June 2026). He and his wife held a full conversation while he talked in his sleep, and neither remembers a word. That is what a lot of AI content feels like: fluent, well formed, and nobody really thought it through.

## Core points
- Fluent is easy now, "we can do it in our sleep, apparently". People even add typos on purpose to seem human.
- AI is not the problem: "The tool did not skip the thinking, the person using it did."
- "Slop is not what AI makes, it is what is left when someone hands over the one part they were supposed to keep": reading, rereading, asking whether it helps the person on the other end.
- Finn does almost all the podcast talking, openly, because the words, angle and research are Bas's.
- His publish test: "Did a human actually think here, or not?" If not, it goes back in the drawer.
- Teased a future topic: "would this actually help the person on the other end".

## Full text (as published)

> Neither of us remembers a word, which is exactly how AI content feels lately.
>
> Last week I told you about how my wife farts in her sleep, and that somehow it made me think of AI. Have a look at the earlier published newsletters if you missed it.
>
> You would think that was the strangest thing our bedroom would ever contribute to my work. Well, it was not.
>
> And don't worry, it's not going to get weird, well, not really.
>
> A few nights ago, I was talking in my sleep, which I apparently do quite a lot.
>
> At some point my wife answered. And before we knew it we held a full conversation, back and forth, complete sentences.
>
> However, neither of us remembered a single word of it. A bit like with a dream, you know it happened, roughly, but the details stay blurry, at best.
>
> Two people talking, coherent (from what we can remember), while neither of us was actually thinking, at least we that's what we "think" now.
>
> And there it was again, the AI thought. Only this time, luckily I wasn't sitting straight up in my bed in the middle of the night.
>
> Because that half asleep conversation is what a lot of content feels like right now, fluent and well formed and grammatically spotless, too spotless, and you can tell within two lines that no one really thought it through before it went out.
>
> It has come to the point where people are even adding typo's and grammor errors, on purpose, to give others the feeling it was a 100% them and not Ai.
>
> (see what I did there?)
>
> The easy take is to say that AI is the problem, that it produces soulless slop. Which, of course, it can do.
>
> But I do not buy that. The tool did not skip the thinking, the person using it did.
>
> Slop is not what AI makes, it is what is left when someone hands over the one part they were supposed to keep. Reading, the rereading, the "would this actually help the person on the other end." Also a great topic for a future newsletter, the latter, I mean.
>
> Nowadays, sounding fluent is easy, we can do it in our sleep, apparently.
>
> Let Ai focus on the delivery, maybe it can help you troubleshoot here and there, get some creative juices flowing, etc… But it cannot, or should not do the "real" thinking for you.
>
> And personally, that is the part I refuse to hand over. Sure, I use Ai, who isn't, but to a certain extend.
>
> For example, on the podcast Finn does almost all the talking, and I am open about that, because the words, the angle and the research are mine, he just delivers. To the point, clear etc, he never gets tired and so on.
>
> The day Finn will do the thinking for me is the day it turns into the bedroom conversation highlighted earlier, smooth on the surface, but kind of empty underneath, and mostly forgotten by morning, or a couple of days later.
>
> And you feel it when you read it as well, whether someone was really there or not, I mean.
>
> So, before anything I write goes out, awake or not, I try to ask one thing... Did a human actually think here, or not? If it is the second one, it goes back in the drawer and ending up not being published, shared etc. at all, potentially.
>
> Anyway, if I ever say something clever in my sleep (doubtful), I hope my wife is paying attention.
>
> It might be my best work, yet :)
>
> BvK.
