# Bas: look critically at 'green' datacenter claims

Bas urges a critical eye on what 'green' actually means, because not all sustainability methods are equally direct or effective. He ranks them by directness:
- Carbon offsetting: buying offsets to neutralise CO2 by financing tree planting, protecting nature, or renewable-energy projects. Reduces CO2 elsewhere but not necessarily the datacenter's own emissions - an indirect way of being green.
- Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs): buying certificates lets a company claim it uses green energy even if its datacenters have no physical access to renewable sources; the energy the datacenter consumes may still be fossil.
- Investing in own renewable sources (solar/wind parks): pulls part of consumption directly from green sources, a much more direct impact and a strong form of sustainability that genuinely reduces fossil-fuel dependence.
- Improving energy efficiency: better cooling, hardware optimisation, AI-driven energy management. Examples include free cooling (using outside air) and liquid cooling (cooling with fluids instead of air). Microsoft's sea-container experiments are a notable case: mini datacenters in a container sunk to the seabed, using cold water to cool. Some datacenters reuse generated heat to warm nearby buildings.

Bas's bottom line: offsetting and certificates are usually less direct than generating own renewables or optimising usage, so some datacenters are genuinely greener than others. As a customer, scrutinise how your provider substantiates its claims and choose those that really invest in renewable energy and efficiency.
