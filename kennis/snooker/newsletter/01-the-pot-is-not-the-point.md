# Edition 01: The pot is not the point

Draft of the series opener, an edition of EUC News Nuggets on LinkedIn. Full BvK voice, English. Not published. Series plan: [README](README.md).

---

**The pot is not the point (what a snooker table taught me about my career)**

This edition of EUC News Nuggets is a bit different from what you are used to, there is no AVD in it, no AI model news and not a single known issue. It is about snooker, the game I picked up when I was 13, and about how much of what I know about learning, work and building a career I picked up at that table, years before I understood that this was what was happening.

I started in a snooker centre in IJmuiden that my uncle and aunt ran, made my first 50 break about six weeks in and my first century after eight or nine months, and from there it got serious: Dutch team titles in the Super League and the lower divisions, a second place at the Dutch Championships A ranking, four seasons in the Dutch top 10, a highest break of 137 and 68 centuries along the way. Then, around the age of 20, work and life took over and the cue went in the case for a long time. Around 2021 I picked it back up, and these days I train a lot again (and yes, I log every session, of course I do).

So here is the idea for this mini-series inside the newsletter. Each one teaches you one thing about the game, so the next time it is on TV you know what you are looking at, and maps it to something in a career, with one thing you can try yourself that week. You do not need to have ever held a cue.

**Snooker in two minutes**

There are 15 red balls on the table, worth one point each, and six colours: yellow (2), green (3), brown (4), blue (5), pink (6) and black (7). When it is your turn you have to pot a red first, then a colour of your choice, then a red again, and so on, and every colour you pot goes back on its spot for as long as there are reds left. Once all the reds are gone, you clear the colours in order, from yellow up to black.

Everything you score in one visit to the table is called a break. The highest possible break, in normal circumstances, is 147, which means all 15 reds with a black after each one, followed by all six colours. Miss, and your opponent comes to the table, and if you hit the wrong ball or pot the white you commit a foul, which hands your opponent at least four points. Being snookered means the white has no straight line to the ball you have to hit, so you need a cushion or a swerve to get there.

That is enough to follow a frame. The rest we pick up along the way.

**The pot is not the point**

This is the one thing almost every beginner gets wrong, and I did too. A beginner watches the ball go into the pocket. A good player barely looks at it, because they are watching where the white ends up, and that is where the game is won or lost.

Every shot in snooker is two jobs at the same time, pot this ball and leave the white in a place where the next ball is easy. Players call that position, and it is the difference between a break of 12 and a break of 80. You can pot beautifully, ball after ball, and still end every visit after two shots because the white keeps rolling to the wrong side of the table.

Think of it this way: the pot is the task in front of you, the ticket, the project, the course you need to deliver this week. The position is where that task leaves you. Most of us are trained to look at the pot, did it go in, is it done, can I tick it off, and far fewer of us stop before the shot and ask where this one leaves us for the next one.

**What that looked like for me**

I started on a helpdesk in 1999, and it took me more than 13 years of self-study (and 60+ certifications, I stopped counting at some point) to grow from there into a senior Citrix engineer. Looking back, the jobs themselves were the pots, and they needed to go in, but a lot of what moved me forward was position play, often without me calling it that.

Starting a blog in 2013 next to my day job paid nothing at the time, yet it left the white in a good place for a book, for community work, for being named a Citrix Technology Professional and later a Microsoft MVP, and in 2020 for becoming the first employee outside the US at the company I still work for, covering EMEA on my own for the first three years. Today I am a Principal Learning Architect, building training for a living, which the 13-year-old in IJmuiden would not have understood at all.

There were plenty of shots where I did not think about position, and you can guess how those visits ended. That is fine, it is how you learn, at the table and at work.

**The player in the chair**

One more thing about the game, because it matters for the rest of this series. When your opponent is at the table, you sit in your chair and you can do nothing, you cannot block, you cannot defend, you can only watch and wait for your turn. The only thing you control is your own visit, so the best players spend the time in the chair on themselves, not on the other player.

I find that a useful way to look at a career too. Somebody else getting the promotion, the speaking slot or the big contract is them at the table, and you staring at it does not change a single ball, so I would rather spend that time on my own frame, the only part of the game I can do anything about.

**What would I do with this?**

Pick one task on your list for this week, before you start it, and write down in one sentence where you want it to leave you. Not whether it gets done, that is the pot, but what it sets up next: a skill you did not have, a person you now know, a piece of work you can point to later. If you cannot think of anything, that is useful information as well, and maybe a reason to play that shot a little differently.

**Coming up**

Next time in this series: the boring routine every serious player does for hours, called colours round the table, and why the parts of your job nobody claps for are the ones that carry you. After that, safety play, getting out of a snooker, missed balls and the art of losing well. The regular nuggets keep coming in between, of course.

Do you play, or did you ever? Or is there another game or sport that taught you more about work than any training did? I would love to hear it, that is often where the next edition starts.
