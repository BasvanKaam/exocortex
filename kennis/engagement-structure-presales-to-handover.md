# Engagement structure: from first conversation to handover

My standard way of running a cloud/workplace engagement, from the first conversation to delivery. Each phase is a gate: you decide go/no-go before spending the next chunk of effort.

## The phases
1. **First introduction / orientation.** Establish the functional question and rough context. Pull in technical pre-sales as early as possible. Whether the solution fits is usually a technical matter (apps, data, network, VPN, user profiles, IaaS, on-prem dependencies), not a sales one, so I don't let it stay a sales-only conversation.
2. **Second session: scope and impact.** Get a high-level picture of the current environment and determine approach and impact. For larger or more complex organisations this can itself be a **paid workshop** rather than free pre-sales. Decide go/no-go for the next phase here.
3. **Proposal (paid).** Write the proposal and lock the agreements. The proposal names who does what (I can cover everything from inventory, design, implementation, management, support), assigns responsibilities, gives an hours estimate / quote, states what gets delivered when (timeline), and offers managed services or handover (a combination is possible). Include a high-level business case / ROI over e.g. 3 years, partly on assumptions.
4. **Decision.** Proposal approved yes/no.
5. **Delivery -> handover and/or managed services.** Build, deliver, then either hand over to the client's own team (with training and temporary guidance) or run it as a managed service. Support can be sold separately on a strip-card / contract basis.

## Principles that make this work
- **Gate, don't sprint.** Each phase ends with an explicit go/no-go so neither side over-invests before fit is confirmed.
- **Charge for the thinking.** Workshop and proposal are paid. Free pre-sales is the qualification, not the design.
- **Don't reinvent the wheel.** Reuse standard inventory documents, an adoption framework, and existing masterclass material rather than building each engagement from scratch.
- **Deliverables don't need to be ten pages.** A focused proposal beats an exhaustive one; "it doesn't have to be completely finished" to be useful at the gate.
- **Small start, no big bang.** Recommend starting small and growing; a proof-of-concept de-risks the decision.
